Coronary stent design and expansion model

I designed a coronary stent in CAD and modelled its balloon expansion and recoil in Abaqus, so that a geometry can be judged before it is made.

Year
2025
Tools
Fusion 360, Abaqus, nonlinear plasticity

The problem

I set this project myself in 2025 to learn Abaqus through a real engineering design problem. The geometry of a coronary stent plays an important role in how it behaves once deployed, so being able to predict its mechanical response early in the design process can help determine whether a design is worth developing further. I wanted to build a model that could reproduce the implantation process, where a balloon expands the stent inside an artery, and assess both the stresses during expansion and the subsequent elastic recoil.

What I did

I created a parametric stent model in Fusion 360 and imported it into Abaqus for a nonlinear analysis including plasticity. I selected cobalt-chromium as the stent material due to its durability, biocompatibility and radiopacity. To keep the analysis computationally manageable, I assumed frictionless contact between the stent and balloon. The expansion was modelled in stages, with 75 % radial expansion used as the reference state, followed by removal of the applied load to capture elastic springback. I also carried out mesh refinement to balance solution accuracy with computational cost while remaining within the 250,000-node limit of the student licence.

Results

The results were mesh-independent to within 5 %, providing confidence that the predicted stresses were representative of the model rather than being strongly dependent on mesh density. The von Mises stress distribution showed that the stent underwent predominantly plastic deformation during expansion. The subsequent springback analysis quantified how much of this deformation was recovered once the balloon was withdrawn. Together, these results provide a useful picture of the stent’s mechanical response during and after deployment, demonstrating how simulation can be used to assess different geometries before committing to manufacture.

Limitations and next steps

The current model assumes frictionless balloon contact and does not include the surrounding artery, so it represents free expansion rather than deployment within a vessel. The student licence also limited the available mesh size to 250,000 nodes. As a next step, I would introduce an artery model with contact friction and compare several strut geometries under consistent meshing and loading conditions.

Piezoelectric scaffolds for muscle regeneration

I fabricated aligned piezoelectric fibre scaffolds, built a rig to test them wet, and showed early signs of muscle cells differentiating on them.

Year
2023 to 2024
Tools
Electrospinning, mechanical testing, cell culture, SEM, confocal microscopy, 3D printing, O2 plasma treatment, ImageJ, Python
Publication
Preprint on bioRxiv (opens in a new tab), in the final stage of review at Small

The problem

When more than 20 % of a muscle’s volume is lost, the body cannot fully regenerate the original tissue on its own. This project investigated a biodegradable scaffold designed to be implanted at the site of injury and guide regenerating muscle towards its original structure and mechanical function. The scaffold is made from PHBV, a piezoelectric polymer that generates a small electrical charge when mechanically deformed. This electromechanical response is intended to provide an environment that more closely resembles the mechanical and electrical cues experienced by muscle cells during normal growth.

What I did

My work covered the experimental development and validation of the scaffold. I produced the fibres using electrospinning and developed a rolling process to assemble them into an aligned, bundled architecture designed to mimic the structure of native muscle. I characterised the mechanical properties of the scaffold under both dry and wet conditions. Since the fibres could not be tested while submerged using the available equipment, I designed and built a dedicated testing rig to enable mechanical characterisation in a wet environment. I also modified the fibre surface to alter its polarity and improve laminin adsorption and cell attachment. Finally, I carried out the biocompatibility experiments and assessed muscle differentiation through myosin staining.

Results

Myosin expression was clearly observed within 5 days, providing an early indication of muscle differentiation on the scaffold rather than simple cell survival. The wet mechanical testing was particularly important because the scaffold’s behaviour in dry conditions would not fully represent its mechanical response in an implanted environment. The work contributed to the preprint Three-Dimensional Piezoelectric Fibrous Constructs for Muscle Regeneration, on which I am a co-author.

Limitations and next steps

The differentiation results remain preliminary, as noted in the preprint. The experiments were conducted in vitro using the C2C12 mouse muscle cell line over a relatively short period, so they do not establish how the scaffold would perform in a living system. The project did not progress to implantation, leaving questions around long-term degradation, tissue integration and mechanical performance under cyclic loading in vivo. The research has since been developed further by subsequent MSc and PhD students.

Rare-earth-free wind turbine generator

I validated in Ansys Maxwell a 2 MW direct-drive generator, re-designed without rare-earth magnets, and showed that it reaches its rating.

Year
2025 to 2026
Tools
Ansys Maxwell, RMxprt, magnetic equivalent circuit

The problem

The wider project investigated whether resonant wireless power transfer could be used to supply the rotor excitation of a direct-drive wind turbine generator. This would allow the machine to replace its rare-earth permanent magnets with a wound rotor, while also removing the need for mechanical slip rings, which are subject to wear. My focus was the generator itself. I needed to determine whether a 2 MW machine redesigned with electrically excited rotor windings could achieve its rated performance and provide the basis for the wider wireless power transfer concept.

What I did

I contributed to the analytical redesign of the generator, sizing the machine using a magnetic equivalent circuit and reluctance network to reproduce the magnetic field of the original permanent-magnet design. I then developed and ran the electromagnetic finite-element simulations in Ansys. RMxprt was used to establish the initial geometry and perform a preliminary performance sweep, followed by a 2D transient Maxwell model for a more detailed analysis of the electromagnetic behaviour. The transient model captured effects including magnetic saturation in the stator teeth, flux leakage and the transient development of the phase currents that could not be fully represented by the analytical model.

Results

The simulated machine reached a peak output of 2.5 MW at a torque angle of approximately 100°, while maintaining an electromagnetic efficiency of 92.7 % across a broad range of operating angles. The predicted three-phase back-EMF and electromagnetic torque showed good agreement with the analytical model. These results demonstrate that the electrically excited design can achieve the required 2 MW rating while maintaining an efficiency comparable to the original permanent-magnet concept. This provided the generator-level validation required for the wider feasibility study.

Limitations and next steps

The electromagnetic analysis was two-dimensional, so end-winding and other axial effects were not captured. The results are also based entirely on simulation, and the reported efficiency accounts only for electromagnetic losses rather than mechanical or converter losses. As a next step, I would extend the analysis to a 3D electromagnetic model and carry out a coupled thermal analysis of the stator, particularly around the teeth where the flux density reaches approximately 2.5 T.

NACA 4412 aerofoil with a Gurney flap

I measured in Fluent how a Gurney flap trades extra lift against extra drag on a NACA 4412 section for vertical-axis wind turbines.

Year
2025 to 2026
Tools
Ansys ICEM CFD, Ansys Fluent, k-omega SST

The problem

Vertical-axis wind turbines operate in relatively low and highly variable wind conditions, making aerodynamic efficiency particularly important. I first characterised the lift and drag of a baseline NACA 4412 aerofoil before investigating a passive modification in the form of a Gurney flap, a small tab positioned at the trailing edge. While a Gurney flap can increase lift, it also increases drag, which reduces the useful aerodynamic power available to the turbine. The objective was therefore not simply to maximise lift, but to understand the trade-off between lift and drag and identify where the modification could provide a net benefit.

What I did

I created the aerofoil geometry and structured C-mesh in Ansys ICEM, extending the computational domain to 20 chord lengths. The leading-edge blocks were converted to splines to maintain good cell orthogonality around the region of highest curvature. Near-wall cells were sized to achieve a target y+ of approximately 1, and mesh quality was assessed against established angle and determinant criteria. I then solved the flow in Fluent using the k-omega SST turbulence model at an angle of attack of 10° and free-stream velocities of 3 and 15 m/s. A mesh independence study, increasing the element count by a factor of √2 between successive meshes, resulted in a final mesh of approximately 78,000 elements.

Results

The grid convergence index for the lift coefficient was 0.037 %, indicating that the reported comparison was not strongly influenced by mesh resolution. The Gurney flap increased the lift coefficient by up to 79 %, but also increased drag by up to 96 % due to changes in the wake behind the trailing edge. The results highlight the importance of optimising flap geometry rather than simply maximising lift. For a wind turbine, the relevant design objective is the balance between the additional lift and associated drag, as this ultimately influences the aerodynamic torque and power produced by the rotor.

Limitations and next steps

The study was two-dimensional and considered only a single angle of attack, whereas a vertical-axis turbine blade experiences a continuously changing angle of attack throughout each rotation. As a next step, I would investigate a range of flap lengths and angles of attack before incorporating the aerodynamic data into a rotating-blade model. This would allow the flap geometry to be evaluated based on its effect on net aerodynamic torque and turbine power rather than lift and drag in isolation.

Paper microfluidic creatinine sensor

I designed paper microfluidic channels for a colour-change creatinine test and tuned their timing in simulation before building paper prototypes.

Year
2024
Tools
MOFEM, Gmsh, Python, paper prototyping

The problem

Creatinine levels in blood increase when the kidneys are unable to clear waste effectively, making low-cost testing methods valuable for accessible health monitoring. This project investigated a paper-based diagnostic device that uses a colour change to indicate creatinine concentration. A sample, buffer, enzyme and stain travel through separate paper channels before reaching a mixing region where they react to produce the test signal. The main engineering challenge was controlling the timing of this process, so that each reagent reached the mixing zone at the correct stage of the reaction.

What I did

I simulated capillary flow through the paper using MOFEM, creating the channel geometries in Gmsh and running transient simulations through Python. This allowed me to track the movement of the fluid front and determine when each reagent reached the mixing region. I initially tested simple geometries to understand isotropic and anisotropic spreading before developing more complex multi-chamber layouts. I iteratively modified the channel geometries based on the simulation results and then fabricated paper prototypes to validate the designs experimentally. I recorded the resulting colour changes using a smartphone camera and compared the experimental behaviour with the simulations.

Results

Using simulation before fabrication allowed me to refine the reagent timing without having to build and test every design physically. The first design introduced the stain too early, at approximately 68 s, while the second overcorrected and delayed it to around 240 s. The third design reduced the timing to approximately 152 s, bringing the system closer to the intended operating window. The physical prototypes supported the main trend predicted by the simulations, with narrower channels producing faster flow. The iterative process also showed that adding geometric features increased the complexity of the flow behaviour, making careful channel design essential for controlling reagent timing.

Limitations and next steps

The prototypes were recorded using a smartphone camera rather than a calibrated optical reader, so the experiments were primarily used to validate the flow behaviour rather than provide quantitative measurements of the colour response. As a next step, I would quantify the colour change using image analysis and test the third design using samples with known creatinine concentrations to establish its sensitivity and measurement range.

Half-hourly electricity demand forecasting

I built a CNN-LSTM and gradient-boosting ensemble to forecast the electricity demand of a UK region at 30 min resolution, 20 steps ahead.

Year
2025
Tools
Python, TensorFlow and Keras, gradient boosting, Bayesian optimisation

The problem

The project required forecasting half-hourly electricity demand for a UK region across the 20 time steps from 00:00 to 09:30 on 30 September 2025. Five years of historical demand data were provided, with model performance evaluated using RMSE against the actual demand. The raw dataset contained several anomalies, including short outages, isolated spikes and a nearly two-month period of constant demand during summer 2024 that was unlikely to represent physical demand behaviour.

What I did

I retained the anomalous periods in the dataset but introduced indicator features to identify them and reduced their influence during training. Isolated spikes were detected using a z-score approach. I then engineered time, calendar and lagged features, including cyclical time encodings, UK public holidays and a flag for the first COVID-19 period. The data were split chronologically, with 90 % used for training and 10 % for testing to avoid introducing information from the future into the model. The final forecast used an ensemble weighted according to validation error, combining a CNN-LSTM model for short-term patterns with gradient boosting for longer-term behaviour. Bayesian optimisation was used to tune the model hyperparameters.

Results

In a one-day backtest using 23 September 2025, one week before the target forecast date, the ensemble achieved an RMSE of 22.6, equivalent to 3.8 % of mean demand. The forecast captured the overall daily demand profile and its major changes in gradient. Analysis of the errors showed larger deviations around 09:30 and 17:00, where demand changes rapidly, as well as on Tuesdays, which was the weekday of the target forecast. The final forward forecast captured the expected overnight increase in demand, followed by a dip and a morning peak at approximately 06:30.

Limitations and next steps

The model tends to smooth local peaks and troughs and can lag behind short-term oscillations, which is a common limitation of sequence-based forecasting models. The residuals also showed a positive bias, indicating a tendency to under-predict demand. As a next step, I would focus feature engineering and sample weighting on the time windows where the model performs least accurately, while averaging predictions from multiple training runs to reduce variation between forecasts.

Drug release from a spherical carrier

I modelled drug diffusion out of a spherical carrier in COMSOL and traced a false early release to the mesh rather than the physics.

Year
2022
Tools
COMSOL Multiphysics, Transport of Diluted Species, mesh convergence

The problem

Controlled-release implants and drug delivery particles rely on controlling how quickly a drug leaves its carrier, since the release profile determines the dose delivered over time. I modelled a drug-loaded spherical carrier surrounded by a thin film to investigate how quickly the drug is released and how the release behaviour changes with the design. The problem is representative of a wider class of controlled-release systems, making it a useful test case for developing and validating a computational modelling workflow.

What I did

I built the spherical geometry in COMSOL and used the Transport of Diluted Species interface to model the drug as a diffusing species. A perfect-sink boundary condition was used to represent surrounding tissue that removes the drug as soon as it reaches the interface. I ran a time-dependent simulation over one hour and tracked both the concentration field and cumulative drug release. Taking advantage of the symmetry of the geometry, I also extracted 1D concentration profiles as a quick check against the full 3D solution before interpreting the results.

Results

The initial release curve showed a cumulative release of approximately 0.19 at time zero, when the expected value was zero. Rather than treating this as a minor numerical artefact, I investigated its origin and traced it to the mesh. With a coarse mesh, the spherical boundary was represented by a faceted surface, which introduced an error in the location of the boundary and therefore the calculated release. Refining the mesh progressively reduced the offset, with the start-point error falling below 5 % at the finest mesh tested. This confirmed that the discrepancy was primarily a numerical effect associated with the geometric representation.

The most valuable outcome was therefore not simply the final release curve, but the verification process used to establish whether the result could be trusted. It reinforced the importance of investigating unexpected numerical behaviour rather than accepting a plausible-looking simulation without understanding its error.

Limitations and next steps

The perfect-sink boundary is an idealisation of the surrounding tissue, and the model was not validated against experimental release data. The initial numerical error was also reduced rather than completely eliminated. As a next step, I would use curved second-order elements to represent the spherical boundary more accurately and compare the simulated release profile with the analytical solution for diffusion from a sphere.

Bed Tower, a bedside power unit

In a team of four, we took a household automation brief to a costed bedside unit; my part was the CAD rendering, the animation and the cost evaluation.

Year
2026
Tools
Fusion 360, Double Diamond, cardboard prototyping, cost estimation

The problem

The brief was to develop a household innovation based on automation that was safe, easy to use, space-efficient, low-maintenance and cost-effective for its intended users. We began by identifying around ten everyday problems from our own homes, narrowed these down to four concepts and selected bedside power as our focus. We identified that conventional bed frames do not adequately accommodate the growing number of electrical devices people use beside the bed, either functionally or aesthetically.

What I did

We used the Double Diamond framework to structure the design process, which suited the open-ended nature of the brief. We developed a persona based on a 30-year-old engineer purchasing his first home to keep the design decisions centred around a specific user. Our initial concept was an integrated addition to the bed frame, but the wide variation in bed frame designs led us to revisit the problem and pivot towards a standalone unit. Further development introduced features including a book holder, cup holder and additional storage. We used cardboard prototypes to test the proportions and position of the unit beside a bed, which led to a final height of 100 cm. My main contributions were the CAD modelling, rendering and animation, as well as the cost analysis.

Results

The final design measures 1000 mm in height, 350 mm in width and 250 mm in depth. It incorporates a gooseneck lamp, UK power socket, USB-A and USB-C charging ports, wireless charging and drawers within a polymer-coated recycled plywood body supported by an aluminium frame. Rendering the unit alongside a king-size bed showed that it could be positioned on either side, while also highlighting a limitation in the lamp’s reach across wider beds. My initial costing estimated the electronics at US$9.22 and the complete unit at US$52.77, compared with commercially available bedside tables that can cost more than US$300.

Limitations and next steps

The costing is an initial estimate based on individual supplier prices rather than production quotations. The design has not yet been developed into a functional prototype, so aspects such as electrical safety, structural loading, durability and long-term usability remain to be tested. In hindsight, the concept also evolved towards a multifunctional bedside product rather than remaining tightly focused on the original automation brief. The next stage would involve user testing across different bedroom layouts and bed heights, a manufacturability review using bulk component pricing and the development of a functional prototype.
