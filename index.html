Coronary stent design and expansion model

I designed a coronary stent in CAD and modelled its balloon expansion and recoil in Abaqus, so that a geometry can be judged before it is made.

Role
Design and analysis, solo
Context
Personal project, undertaken to learn Abaqus
Year
2025
Tools
Fusion 360, Abaqus, nonlinear plasticity
  • < 5 %mesh-independence error, so the stresses follow the physics rather than the mesh
  • Recoil after balloon removal measured in a separate springback step.

The problem

I set this project myself in 2025, to learn Abaqus on a real design question. The geometry of a coronary stent is the main factor in how it performs once deployed. Predicting that behaviour early therefore decides whether a design is worth making. I wanted a model that reproduces implantation, when a balloon expands the stent inside an artery, and that reports both the stress during expansion and the recoil afterwards.

What I did

I built the stent as a parametric model in Fusion 360 and imported it into Abaqus for a nonlinear analysis with plasticity. The material was cobalt-chromium, chosen for its durability, biocompatibility and radiopacity. I assumed frictionless contact at the balloon interface to keep the run tractable. Expansion was solved in stages, with 75 % radial expansion as the reference state, and a second step removed the load to capture elastic springback. I refined the mesh to balance accuracy against run time, within the 250,000-node limit of the student licence.

What came out of it

The solution was mesh-independent to within 5 %, which gives confidence that the stresses reflect the physics rather than the mesh. The von Mises results showed the stent deforming almost entirely plastically during expansion. The springback step then measured how much of that deformation recovers once the balloon is withdrawn. Together, these describe the stent in service, and they show that the model can screen geometries before manufacture, where most of the cost and risk sit.

Limitations and what I would do next

The balloon contact was frictionless and the model has no artery wall, so it describes free expansion rather than deployment in a vessel. The student licence also capped the mesh at 250,000 nodes. Next, I would add an artery model and contact friction, and then compare two or three strut geometries under identical mesh settings.

Piezoelectric scaffolds for muscle regeneration

I fabricated aligned piezoelectric fibre scaffolds, built a rig to test them wet, and showed early signs of muscle cells differentiating on them.

Role
Team project. My part covered fabrication, mechanical testing including the wet rig, biocompatibility and differentiation assays.
Context
BEng thesis, Advanced Research Centre, University of Glasgow
Year
2023 to 2024
Publication
Preprint on bioRxiv (opens in a new tab), in the final stage of review at Small
Tools
Electrospinning, mechanical testing, cell culture, SEM, confocal microscopy, 3D printing, O2 plasma treatment, ImageJ, Python
  • 5 daysto clear myosin expression, an early sign that the cells were differentiating into muscle
  • 2nd of 12authors on the bioRxiv preprint
  • A tensile rig designed and built to test fibres submerged, which the group could not do before.

The problem

Patients who lose more than 20 % of a muscle’s volume cannot regrow it on their own. This project explores a biodegradable scaffold, implanted at the injury, that guides regrowing muscle back to its original geometry and mechanical behaviour. The scaffold is made from PHBV, a polymer that produces a small electrical charge when it is deformed. That charge recreates the electromechanical environment in which muscle cells normally grow.

What I did

My work covered the whole validation. I produced the fibres by electrospinning, then designed a rolling protocol that gathers them into the aligned, bundled architecture of real muscle. I measured their mechanical properties dry and wet. There was no way to test fibres submerged, so I designed and built the rig. I treated the fibres to change their surface polarity, which improves laminin absorption and cell adherence. Finally, I ran the biocompatibility work and stained for myosin, the protein that marks muscle differentiation.

What came out of it

Clear myosin expression appeared within 5 days, which suggests that the cells were differentiating into muscle on the scaffold rather than only surviving on it. That result depended on the wet rig, since dry data alone would not describe the scaffold once implanted. The findings form part of the preprint “Three-Dimensional Piezoelectric Fibrous Constructs for Muscle Regeneration”, on which I am the second of twelve authors.

Limitations and what I would do next

The differentiation results are preliminary, as the preprint itself states. They come from in vitro culture of a mouse cell line, C2C12, over a short period. The work did not reach implantation, so questions such as how the scaffold degrades under cyclic load in the body remain open. The investigation has since been taken further by master’s and PhD students.

Rare-earth-free wind turbine generator

I validated in Ansys Maxwell a 2 MW direct-drive generator, re-designed without rare-earth magnets, and showed that it reaches its rating.

Role
All Maxwell and RMxprt simulation, and part of the analytical re-design, in a team of four
Context
Group project, MSc Advanced Mechanical Engineering, Cranfield University
Year
2025 to 2026
Tools
Ansys Maxwell, RMxprt, magnetic equivalent circuit
  • 2.5 MWpeak output, above the 2 MW rating, without rare-earth magnets
  • 92.7 %electromagnetic efficiency across a wide range of operating angles
  • Back-EMF and torque agree with the analytical model.

The problem

The wider project asked whether resonant wireless power transfer could supply the rotor excitation of a direct-drive wind turbine generator. That would let the machine drop its rare-earth permanent magnets, together with the slip rings that wear out. My part was the generator. I had to show that a 2 MW machine, re-designed with wound rotor excitation in place of the magnets, reaches its rated performance, because the feasibility case fails otherwise.

What I did

I contributed to the analytical re-design, sizing the machine with a magnetic equivalent circuit and reluctance network so that the wound rotor reproduced the field of the permanent-magnet design. I then built and ran all of the electromagnetic finite-element work in Ansys. RMxprt gave the initial geometry and performance sweep, and a 2D transient Maxwell model gave the detailed field solution. The transient model captured saturation in the stator teeth, flux leakage and the start-up of the phase currents, which the analytical work could not.

What came out of it

The machine reached a peak output of 2.5 MW near a torque angle of 100°, and held an electromagnetic efficiency of 92.7 % across a wide range of operating angles. Its three-phase back-EMF and electromagnetic torque agreed with the analytical predictions. Hence the electrically excited machine can reach its 2 MW rating at an efficiency competitive with the permanent-magnet design it replaces. The rest of the feasibility study depended on that result.

Limitations and what I would do next

The field solution was two-dimensional, so end-winding and axial effects were not modelled, and the results are from simulation alone. The efficiency is electromagnetic only, so mechanical and converter losses sit outside it. Next, I would run a 3D or coupled thermal check on the stator teeth, where the flux density peaks at about 2.5 T.

Bed Tower, a bedside power unit

In a team of four, we took a household automation brief to a costed bedside unit; my part was the CAD rendering, the animation and the cost evaluation.

Role
CAD rendering and animation, cost evaluation, in a team of four
Context
Component Design module, MSc Advanced Mechanical Engineering, Cranfield University
Year
2026
Tools
Fusion 360, Double Diamond, cardboard prototyping, cost estimation
  • 3cardboard models, built to settle the layout and the height
  • 100 cmfinal height, chosen after trials at 50, 100 and 120 cm
  • US$9.22electronics bill of materials at volume prices
  • US$52.77estimated manufacturing cost per unit, materials plus 10 %

The problem

The brief was to create an innovation based on automation in the household. It had to be safe and easy to use, fit the space in a home, need no maintenance, suit its target users and be cost effective. We listed about ten real problems from our own homes, narrowed them to four concepts, and chose bedside power. Our problem statement was that existing bed frames do not support the electrical devices people now keep by the bed, either functionally or attractively.

What I did

We followed the Double Diamond framework, which suited an open brief better than the Stanford method. A persona, a 30-year-old engineer who has just bought his first home, kept decisions tied to one user. The first concept was an add-on to the bed frame, but frames vary too much, so we returned to the problem statement and pivoted to a standalone unit. A storyboard then added a book holder, a cup holder and better drawers. Cardboard models tested beside a bed set the height at 100 cm. My part was the CAD rendering and animation and the cost evaluation.

What came out of it

The final design is 1000 mm tall, 350 mm wide and 250 mm deep. It carries a gooseneck lamp, a UK socket, USB-A and USB-C ports, wireless charging and drawers, in a polymer-coated recycled plywood body on an aluminium frame. Rendering it beside a king-size bed showed that it suits either side of the bed, and also exposed a limit: the lamp does not reach across a very wide bed. My costing put the electronics at US$9.22 and the whole unit at US$52.77, whereas bedside tables can sell for more than US$300.

Limitations and what I would do next

The costing is a first estimate built from supplier unit prices, not a quotation. The unit has not been built as a working prototype or tested for safety, load or lifespan. In hindsight, the concept also drifted towards a multi-function product rather than answering the automation brief tightly. Next would come user tests across bedroom layouts and bed heights, a manufacturability review with bulk pricing, and a full prototype.

NACA 4412 aerofoil with a Gurney flap

I measured in Fluent how a Gurney flap trades extra lift against extra drag on a NACA 4412 section for vertical-axis wind turbines.

Role
Individual
Context
Computational Fluid Dynamics for Renewable Energy module, MSc Advanced Mechanical Engineering, Cranfield University
Year
2025 to 2026
Tools
Ansys ICEM CFD, Ansys Fluent, k-omega SST
  • 0.037 %grid convergence index on the lift coefficient
  • +79 %lift coefficient with the Gurney flap, at most
  • +96 %drag with the flap, at most, which is the penalty that decides the trade-off

The problem

Vertical-axis wind turbines work in low, gusty urban wind, so their blade sections need all the lift they can get. I first quantified the lift and drag of a baseline NACA 4412 section. I then tested a passive modification, a Gurney flap, which is a small tab at the trailing edge. The flap raises lift but also raises drag, and on a turbine any extra drag is power that is never generated. The question is therefore one of tuning rather than simple improvement.

What I did

I built the geometry and a structured C-mesh in Ansys ICEM, with the domain extended to 20 chord lengths. The leading-edge blocks were converted to splines to keep cells orthogonal where the curvature is sharpest. Wall cells were sized for a y+ of 1, and mesh quality was checked against accepted angle and determinant limits. I solved the flow in Fluent with the k-omega SST model at a 10° angle of attack, for free-stream speeds of 3 and 15 m/s. A mesh study, scaling the element count by √2 at each step, settled on 78,000 elements.

What came out of it

The grid convergence index on the lift coefficient was 0.037 %, so the comparison rests on the physics rather than on the mesh. The Gurney flap raised the lift coefficient by up to 79 %. However, it raised drag by up to 96 %, by thickening the wake behind the trailing edge. The design conclusion is that flap length must be tuned to where the lift gain still outweighs the drag penalty. That balance sets the net torque and power of a real turbine.

Limitations and what I would do next

The study was two-dimensional and used a single 10° angle of attack, whereas a vertical-axis blade passes through a full range of angles in every rotation. Next, I would sweep flap length and angle of attack together, then carry the results into a rotating-blade model to find the flap length that gives the most net torque.

Paper microfluidic creatinine sensor

I designed paper microfluidic channels for a colour-change creatinine test and tuned their timing in simulation before building paper prototypes.

Role
Individual
Context
BEng Biomedical Engineering, University of Glasgow
Year
2024
Tools
MOFEM, Gmsh, Python, paper prototyping
  • 68 sstain arrival in the first design, far too early
  • 152 sstain arrival in the third design, after an overcorrection to 240 s in the second
  • Narrower channels flowed faster in the paper prototypes, as the simulation predicted.

The problem

Creatinine in blood rises when the kidneys stop clearing waste properly, so a cheap paper test for it is useful. The device works by colour: sample, buffer, enzyme and stain wick through paper channels, mix and react to give a reading. The difficult part is timing, because the stain has to meet the reacted sample inside a narrow window. The real design task is therefore to shape the channels so that each reagent arrives at the right moment.

What I did

I simulated capillary flow through the paper in MOFEM, building each channel geometry in Gmsh and running the transient flow from Python. That let me watch the fluid front advance and see when each reagent reached the mixing zone. I started with simple shapes to understand isotropic and anisotropic spreading, then designed my own multi-chamber layouts and reshaped them after each run. Finally, I made the designs as paper prototypes and recorded the colour change with a phone camera for comparison with the simulations.

What came out of it

Simulating first let me tune the timing before building anything. The first design brought the stain in far too early, at about 68 s, and the second overcorrected to 240 s. The third reached about 152 s as the channels closed in on the target. The paper prototypes confirmed the main trend, with narrower channels giving faster flow. The design lesson was that every added geometric feature made the flow harder to predict, so the timing had to be earned through iteration.

Limitations and what I would do next

The prototypes were recorded with a phone camera rather than a calibrated reader, and they confirmed the flow trend rather than the exact timings. Next, I would measure the colour change quantitatively and test the third design against samples of known creatinine concentration.

Half-hourly electricity demand forecasting

I built a CNN-LSTM and gradient-boosting ensemble to forecast the electricity demand of a UK region at 30 min resolution, 20 steps ahead.

Role
Individual
Context
Artificial Intelligence for Sustainability module, MSc Advanced Mechanical Engineering, Cranfield University
Year
2025
Tools
Python, TensorFlow and Keras, gradient boosting, Bayesian optimisation
  • 22.6RMSE on a one-day backtest for 23 September 2025, which is 3.8 % of mean demand
  • 90 / 10time-ordered split between training and test data, so no future data leaks into training
  • 20 stepsforecast at 30 min resolution, from 00:00 to 09:30 on 30 September 2025

The problem

The module asked for a model that forecasts the half-hourly electricity demand of a UK region for the 20 steps from 00:00 to 09:30 on 30 September 2025. Five years of history were provided, and accuracy was judged by RMSE against the real demand. The raw series was not clean. It contained short outages and isolated spikes, as well as a flat period of nearly two months in summer 2024 that could not be physically real.

What I did

I kept the outages and the flat period in the series, but marked them with indicator features and down-weighted them in training. Spikes were flagged with a z-score test. I added time, calendar and lagged features, including cyclical encodings, UK public holidays and a flag for the first Covid period. The data were split in time order, 90 % for training and 10 % for testing. The forecast came from an ensemble weighted by validation error: a CNN-LSTM network for the short horizon and gradient boosting for the longer one. Bayesian optimisation tuned the hyperparameters.

What came out of it

On a one-day backtest for 23 September 2025, a week before the target date, the model reached an RMSE of 22.6, which is 3.8 % of mean demand. It follows the overall shape of demand and its changes of gradient well. The error heat map shows where it struggles: around 09:30 and 17:00, when demand turns, and on Tuesdays, the weekday being forecast. The forward forecast shows the expected overnight rise, then a dip and a morning peak near 06:30.

Limitations and what I would do next

The model smooths local peaks and troughs and lags short oscillations, which is a known behaviour of LSTM-based models. Its residuals also lean positive, so it tends to under-predict. Next, I would target the difficult time windows with better features and sample weighting, and average several training runs to reduce the variation between forecasts.

Drug release from a spherical carrier

I modelled drug diffusion out of a spherical carrier in COMSOL and traced a false early release to the mesh rather than the physics.

Role
Individual
Context
BEng Biomedical Engineering, University of Glasgow
Year
2022
Tools
COMSOL Multiphysics, Transport of Diluted Species, mesh convergence
  • < 5 %start-point error after mesh refinement, down from an offset of 0.19 at t = 0
  • The offset was traced to faceting of the sphere by a coarse mesh.

The problem

Controlled-release implants and particles depend on how quickly a drug leaves its carrier, because the release profile sets the dose a patient receives over time. I modelled a monolithic sphere loaded with drug and wrapped in a thin film. The questions were how fast the drug leaves and how the release rate depends on the design. The same problem sits behind many drug delivery devices, which made it a good test of a modelling workflow.

What I did

I built the sphere in COMSOL and used the Transport of Diluted Species interface to model the drug as a diffusing species. A perfect-sink boundary stood for tissue that removes the drug as fast as it arrives. I ran a time-dependent study to one hour and tracked the concentration field and the cumulative release. Because the geometry is symmetric, I used 1D line profiles as a quick check against the full 3D field before trusting the result.

What came out of it

The release curve read about 0.19 at time zero, where it should read zero. Rather than ignore it, I traced it to the mesh: a coarse mesh represents the sphere as a faceted surface, which misplaces the boundary. Refining the mesh in steps shrank the offset, and at a very fine mesh the start-point error fell below 5 %. That confirmed the cause was numerical. The lasting outcome was a habit of not accepting a clean-looking result until I understand where its error comes from.

Limitations and what I would do next

A perfect sink is an idealisation of tissue, and the model was not compared with measured release data. The start-point error was reduced rather than removed. Next, I would mesh the sphere with curved second-order elements and compare the release curve with the analytical solution for diffusion from a sphere.

CIFAR-10 image classification

Deep learning coursework, TensorFlow and Keras.

  • 77 %validation accuracy, convolutional network
  • 55 %validation accuracy, network trained on PCA-reduced inputs
  • 52 %validation accuracy, fully connected network

I built and compared three classifiers for the CIFAR-10 image set: a fully connected network, a convolutional network and a network trained on PCA-reduced inputs. The convolutional model reached a validation accuracy of about 77 %, against about 52 % for the dense network and 55 % for the PCA version. The gap shows how much convolution helps when the spatial structure of an image matters.

Image segmentation and object counting

Image analysis coursework, scikit-image, SciPy and NumPy.

Two pieces of computer vision work. The first separates an image into its colour channels, downsamples it and plots intensity histograms to show how it is made up. The second is a segmentation pipeline that thresholds an image and cleans the masks with morphological opening and closing. It then labels connected regions to count the objects and find their centroids.

Filter design for biosignals

Signal Processing of Biosignatures module, University of Glasgow.

Frequency-domain analysis and filter design in Python. I used the discrete Fourier transform to recover known tones buried in noise, then designed and compared FIR and IIR filters for low-pass, high-pass and band-pass cases. The FIR designs used Hamming and Hann windows, and the IIR designs used Butterworth and Chebyshev forms.

UK healthcare expenditure charts

Data visualisation coursework, BEng Biomedical Engineering, University of Glasgow.

Three charts of UK healthcare spending, re-plotted for this site in one consistent style from the Office for National Statistics tables. A broken-axis line chart shows the public and private shares from 1997 to 2013, which move as mirror images. The public share rose by 2.5 percentage points between 2007 and 2009, partly because the recession reduced private spending. A grouped bar chart shows the same split in absolute terms, and a bar chart ranks household spending in 2013 by type of service. The original assignment used a pie chart for that last breakdown, but seven categories are easier to compare as bars.
